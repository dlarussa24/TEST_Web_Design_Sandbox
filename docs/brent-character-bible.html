Character Bible v1.0 Faceless · Voiceless · Rung 01

Ask for Brent

A Canada goose in a customer-facing role. He is confident, he is wrong, and he would like to speak to you about the returns policy.

Why the premise is built this way

The comedy engine is total self-assurance meeting total incompetence, with menace underneath. Brent does not know he is bad at the job. That single decision generates infinite episodes, gives every image a reason to exist beyond "goose standing up," and — because he is always at work — supplies a continuity prop that solves three problems at once. See § 03.

Recommendation: BRENT, with the wordmark ASK FOR BRENT. A bare first name is a weak mark — common personal names are hard to protect. A phrase is not: Ask for Brent is distinctive, it is what you say at a service desk, and in context it reads as a mild threat. It also works as the handle, the merch line, and the sign-off on every caption.

Do this in week one

The name and mark are the entire moat. AI output carries no copyright, so trademark is the only protection available to you — and trademark rights build from use in commerce, which starts the day you post. Use the mark consistently from post one, keep dated records, and look at a registration once the account has traction.

02

Model sheet — the canon lock

Consistency across generations is the single hardest technical problem in this format, and it is solved by discipline rather than by tooling. Everything below is fixed forever. If it changes, it is not Brent.

Canon pose · three-quarter · eye level
SpeciesCanada goose. Chosen for the black head and white chinstrap — the highest-contrast head marking of any goose, which is what survives the 120px thumbnail test.
PostureFully upright, weight even, shoulders square to camera. Never crouching, never mid-flap. He stands like a man waiting for a lift.
ArmsWings read as arms with a clear elbow. The transgression is arms alone — he was already bipedal, so do not also add hands with fingers. Wingtips, not fingers.
WardrobeHi-vis vest, always. Two reflective bands, open front. It is the silhouette anchor and it survives any setting.
PropLanyard and name badge, always. See § 03 — this is the most important object in the entire brand.
ExpressionFlat. Unblinking. Brent never emotes. All the acting is done by the neck and the situation. This is the deadpan rule and it is non-negotiable.
ScaleRoughly one metre tall — smaller than the humans around him, which is funnier and keeps him unthreatening in frame.
NeverNo weapons. No clothing beyond the vest. No speech bubbles. No sunglasses. No human hands.

03

The badge — one prop, three problems

StaffNo. 001
BRENT
Ask for me
Employee of the month

The lanyard badge is the highest-leverage design decision in this bible, because one object does three jobs no other prop can:

  • Continuity anchor. Image models drift. A high-contrast rectangular object in a fixed position is far easier to hold stable across generations than subtle anatomy, and its presence instantly tells a viewer this is the same character even when the render wobbles.
  • Wordmark carrier. Your trademark appears inside the artwork, in every single post, without a watermark. It cannot be cropped out without destroying the image — and unlike a corner logo, it is diegetic, so it never reads as branding.
  • Premise engine. The role line changes with each episode. That one text field is the whole series format, and it is the thing your audience will start predicting in the comments.

Rule: the badge is always visible and always legible. If a composition hides it, the composition is wrong.

04

Prompt scaffold

Four blocks. Three of them never change — copy them verbatim every time. Editing the character block is how characters drift, and drift is how a property becomes a stock image.

Block A — character · never edit
A Canada goose standing fully upright on two legs, about one metre tall,
with a black head and neck, a crisp white chinstrap marking, and a warm
brown body. Its wings are held like human arms with a visible elbow bend,
ending in wingtips — not hands, no fingers. It wears an open high-visibility
yellow-green safety vest with two dark reflective bands across the chest,
and a lanyard with a small white rectangular name badge on its chest. Its
expression is completely flat and unblinking. Orange webbed feet.
Block B — scene · the only part you change
It is standing behind the counter of a suburban garden centre, holding a
clipboard, while a confused customer gestures at a dead plant. Mid-morning.
Block C — camera · never edit
Shot on a phone camera, candid, slightly imperfect framing, natural
available light, shallow depth of field, subtle motion blur, 4:5 vertical.
Photographic realism, documentary feel, no stylisation. Eye-level, medium
shot, the goose in the middle third of the frame with its badge legible.
Block D — negative · never edit
human hands, fingers, cartoon, illustration, 3D render, smiling, open beak,
teeth, sunglasses, clothing other than the vest, weapons, text overlay,
speech bubble, watermark, wings spread, flying, crouching, multiple geese
Beyond the prompt

Text alone will not hold a character. Generate a reference sheet first — front, three-quarter, profile, and a close-up of the badge — then use it as a character or style reference on every subsequent image. Keep the best output as the canonical reference and re-anchor to it whenever drift creeps in. That reference sheet is also your evidence of creative control if you ever want to argue human authorship over a composite.

The one prompt discipline that matters legally

Block C asks for a generated photographic scene, not an edit of a real photograph. Do not feed real street photos in as a base — that is the derivative-work trap. If you want real backgrounds, shoot them yourself and composite; that also gives you a genuine human contribution to claim.

The capybara's correct job

Episode 08 gives the capybara the role it is actually built for: the colleague who never reacts. Its meme equity is enormous and its personality is passive, which makes it a terrible protagonist and a perfect foil. Brent generates the chaos; the capybara declines to acknowledge it. You get the capybara's reach without asking it to carry a series.

06

Voice, captions, and the comment engine

Captions are written as internal corporate communication, never as narration. Brent's world has memos, not jokes. This is what keeps the account from reading as an image dump, and it is your original written contribution — the human authorship that matters for both monetization eligibility and any copyright claim over the composite.

Caption formula
[Flat operational statement, present tense, no punchline]
[One escalating detail delivered with total neutrality]

Ask for Brent.

— optional: the vote line, e.g. "Next shift: air traffic control or
   passport control? Comments decide."
  • Never explain the joke. The image is the joke; the caption is the paperwork.
  • Never break character. No "lol", no emoji, no meta-commentary about AI.
  • The vote line is your comment engine. Reference posts in this genre run comment-to-like ratios around 0.002. Asking the audience to choose the next job is the cheapest available fix, it needs no face and no voice, and it creates the continuity that turns viewers into followers.
  • "Ask for Brent" closes every caption. Repetition is what builds a mark.
  • Label the post as AI at upload. The label is not a ranking penalty, and concealment is the thing that actually costs you.
07

Production rules

  1. Generate the reference sheet before episode one. Nothing ships until Brent is stable across four angles.
  2. One variable per image. Change the scene block only. When output drifts, re-anchor to the reference rather than patching the character block.
  3. Vary composition deliberately — interior and exterior, close and wide, with and without humans. Formula repetition is the specific behaviour that degrades recommendation and monetization, so treat variance as a compliance control, not a preference.
  4. Backgrounds generated or self-shot. Never composite over someone else's photograph.
  5. Log every post — date, prompt version, reference image used, badge role. This is your source log, adapted: it evidences the human creative control behind each image.
  6. Three a week beats ten a week if ten means repeating the same frame. The reference accounts' weakness is sameness; that is the gap you are attacking.
