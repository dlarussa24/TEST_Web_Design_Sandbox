Character Bible v2 Photoreal Supersedes the goose

It Wasn't Rufus

A photographically real raccoon, caught mid-act in a real place, entirely unbothered that you are filming. No costume, no styling, no mascot — the animal is exactly as it is, and the situation is the only thing that is wrong.

What changed and why

Under an ultra-realistic brief the ranking inverts. I previously scored raccoon down on impossibility, because raccoons already grasp things — but that reasoning optimized for conceptual absurdity while ignoring what actually determines whether a photoreal image succeeds. Render fidelity dominates. A goose cannot grasp, so every prompt asking one to hold an object forces the model to invent anatomy it has no reference for — and the gap gets filled with human-hand artifacts, the single most recognizable AI tell there is. Raccoon paws are genuinely hand-like and abundantly photographed doing exactly that. You get impossibility of behaviour without ever asking for impossibility of anatomy.

Raccoon, decisively. The goose was the better answer to the question I was asking; the raccoon is the better answer to the question you are actually asking.

What is lost, and the replacement

The badge is gone — a costume prop cannot survive a photoreal brief. It was solving three real problems, so each needs a native replacement: continuity now comes from a permanent physical marking, the wordmark moves out of the artwork into the handle, caption and merch, and the per-episode premise engine becomes the act he is caught performing. See § 02 and § 03.

02

The canon — identity without costume

Everything below is fixed forever. The markers are the only way a viewer knows one photoreal raccoon from another, so they matter more here than any design decision in the previous version.

Marker diagram · not artwork
Marker 01Notched left ear. A clean wedge missing from the upper-left ear. Highest-contrast marker, reads in silhouette, survives any lighting, and is easy for a model to hold. This is the primary identifier.
Marker 02Broken mask bridge. The dark mask does not connect across the nose on the left side. Subtle, but it is what makes close-ups verifiably him.
Marker 03Pale streak over the right brow. A small patch of near-white fur. Reads best in flash and streetlight.
Marker 04Six tail rings. Always six. Nobody counts consciously; everybody notices when it changes.
AnatomyA real raccoon. Full stop. Correct proportions, correct fur, correct paws. Never anthropomorphised limbs, never human hands, never a humanoid torso.
The actHe stands on his hind legs and manipulates objects with his front paws — both of which real raccoons do. The impossibility is the behaviour and the context, never the body.
SignatureDirect eye contact with the lens, every single image. He has seen you. He has not stopped. This is the replacement for the badge and it is the whole joke.
NeverNo clothing. No accessories worn. No weapons. No speech bubbles. No cartoon proportions. No studio lighting. No smiling.
03

Two identities, one raccoon

Rufus runs in two modes. Same animal, same markers, same deadpan — what changes is whether he knows. That single variable is the character's whole arc, and it turns a gag account into a serial.

Why the two marks are the system

They are a matched pair, and each only works in its own mode. Ask for Rufus is what you say at a service desk — sincere on its face, a mild threat once you know him. It wasn't Rufus is a flat denial published directly beneath photographic evidence to the contrary, which only lands once he is actually committing things. Both are distinctive phrases rather than bare first names, which is what makes them protectable, and running both doubles your merch surface off one character.

The pivot is your tentpole

Somewhere around post thirty, Rufus realizes. That single image — the moment the confidence drops out of the face — is the most important post the account will ever make, and everything before it becomes setup in retrospect. Plan it, do not stumble into it. Afterwards the two modes alternate rather than replacing each other: the community man still turns up, and the question of which Rufus you are getting becomes the thing people follow for.

The mechanic worth exploiting

Most images work in either voice, and the caption alone decides which. The ATM frame captioned "assisting a customer with a routine transaction" is the community man; the same frame captioned "I'm inside" is Rogue. Once the audience understands both modes, posting an ambiguous image and letting the comments argue over which Rufus this is costs you nothing and generates exactly the discussion this format otherwise cannot buy.

Where the marks live

With the badge gone the wordmark cannot sit inside the artwork, and it should not — a watermark on a photoreal image reads as stock. Both marks live in the handle, the caption sign-off, and merchandise, which is how essentially all character IP works. Trademark rights build from use in commerce, not from the mark appearing in the picture. Over time the notched ear becomes the visual identifier, and a consistently used distinctive marking is the kind of thing that can mature into protectable trade dress.

04

Prompt scaffold

Four blocks. Only Block B changes. Block C is doing more work than any other part of this document — under a photoreal brief, the camera is what separates a convincing snapshot from an obvious render.

Block A — subject · never edit
A real North American raccoon, photographed, anatomically accurate, normal
raccoon proportions and size. Dense grey-brown fur, black facial mask, pale
muzzle, ringed bushy tail with six dark rings. A distinctive clean wedge
notch missing from the upper part of its LEFT ear. The dark mask does not
connect across the bridge of its nose on the left side. A small pale, almost
white streak of fur above its right brow. It is standing up on its hind legs
and using its front paws, as raccoons naturally do. It is looking directly
into the camera lens.
Block B — the act · the only part you change
It is standing at an outdoor ATM at night, front paws on the keypad, mid-
transaction, turning its head to look at the camera. A queue of two people
waits behind it.
Block C — camera · never edit · does the heavy lifting
SHOT AT NIGHT, IN THE DARK. The only light sources are an orange sodium
streetlamp overhead and the glow of a screen. Deep shadows, crushed blacks,
heavy orange-and-cyan colour cast. NOT daylight. NOT indoors under clean
lighting.

A bad candid smartphone photograph, handheld, taken in a hurry by someone
who was not ready. Crooked horizon, subject off-centre, awkward crop.
Noticeable motion blur, heavy sensor noise and colour speckle in the
shadows, slight lens smear, blown highlights where the light hits.
Imperfect focus — softer than it should be. 4:5 vertical.

It should look like a mediocre photo a passer-by actually took and posted,
not a good photograph of an animal.
Block D — negative · never edit
human hands, fingers, anthropomorphic, humanoid body, cartoon, illustration,
3D render, CGI, plush toy, clothing, hat, vest, accessories, weapons,
smiling, teeth, studio lighting, softbox, professional photography, sharp
clean image, symmetrical framing, text, watermark, logo, oversized raccoon,
multiple raccoons
Findings from generation 001 — the ATM, soul_2, seed 525496

The thesis held; the details did not. Paws rendered as real raccoon paws with claws on the keypad — no hands, no fingers — which is the whole reason this character beat the goose. Bipedal stance, lens eye contact, correct scale against the ATM and the queue: all landed. But three of four markers were dropped (mask break, pale brow, and a notch that came back ambiguous at best), and Block C was ignored outright — the model returned a bright, clean, well-composed daytime interior instead of a night street.

Two lessons, both now folded into this document. Markers will not survive a long text prompt — detail buried mid-paragraph gets averaged away, which is exactly why the reference-image chain below is not optional. And models default to making a good photograph, so the camera block has to be aggressive and negative about it; the version above was rewritten after this generation to lead with darkness and ask explicitly for a mediocre image.

The three failure modes to watch for

Human hands — the instant tell; reject the generation, never retouch it. Scale drift — models inflate him toward human size; he is a normal raccoon, roughly knee height. Over-polish — if it looks like a good photograph, it has failed. The reference posts in this genre succeed partly because they look slightly bad.

The one rule with legal weight

Block C asks for a generated scene, not an edit of a real photograph. Never feed a real street photo in as a base — that makes your output a derivative work of someone else's picture and forfeits every advantage this format has. If you want genuine locations, shoot the plates yourself and composite; that also creates real human authorship in the finished image, which is the only thing you can claim, since AI output alone carries no copyright.

Two structural notes

Episode 07 establishes a second visual register — grainy CCTV — which gives you variety without breaking realism, and doubles as proof for the running "it wasn't him" bit. Episode 13 gives the capybara its correct job: the foil that never reacts. Its meme equity is enormous and its personality is passive, which makes it a poor lead and a perfect witness.

06

Captions and the comment engine

Captions are written as denial and eyewitness testimony — never as narration or jokes. The image is the evidence; the caption is the alibi. This is also your original written contribution, which is the human authorship that matters for monetization eligibility.

Formula A — Rufus the Raccoon
[A civic bulletin. Present tense. He is reporting on his own good work.]
[One procedural detail he is proud of that the photograph flatly contradicts.]
[Optional: unsolicited advice for the public.]

Ask for Rufus.
Formula B — Rufus Gone Rogue
[One short line. Operational. No context, no explanation, no adjectives.]
[Optional second line — shorter than the first.]

It wasn't Rufus.

The same frame, both voices

Worked from the ATM image, to show how far the caption moves the meaning without touching the picture:

ATM · community man
Assisting a customer with a routine withdrawal this morning. Card was
inserted the wrong way round twice, which is a very common error and
nothing to be embarrassed about.

ATM · gone rogue
Third machine this week.

They were fine with me handling their money for eleven years.

It wasn't Rufus.

Rules that hold across both

  • Never explain the joke. The photo is the joke. The caption is either the alibi or the report.
  • Never break character. No emoji, no "lol", no meta-commentary about AI inside the caption.
  • Length is the tell. The community man over-explains because he thinks he did well. Rogue says as little as possible. If a Rogue caption runs past two lines, it is a community-man caption wearing the wrong sign-off.
  • He is never cruel and never armed. Rogue's revenge is procedural and property-scale — systems, machines, paperwork. Never a person, never a weapon; that is the brand-safety line and it does not move.
  • Sign-offs are load-bearing. Every caption ends with its mode's mark, verbatim, every time. Repetition is what builds a mark.
  • The vote line is the comment engine. Reference accounts in this genre run comment-to-like ratios around 0.002 — enormous reach, no community. Ask which mode people want next, or where he turns up. It needs no face and no voice.
  • Label the post as AI at upload. The label carries no ranking penalty; concealment is what costs you.
  • Alternate deliberately. Running the two modes in rotation is not only a story decision — it is the compositional variance that keeps you out of the mass-produced-formula bucket.
