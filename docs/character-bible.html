Character Bible v2 Photoreal Supersedes the goose

It Wasn't Rufus

A photographically real raccoon, caught mid-act in a real place, entirely unbothered that you are filming. No costume, no styling, no mascot — the animal is exactly as it is, and the situation is the only thing that is wrong.

What changed and why

Under an ultra-realistic brief the ranking inverts. I previously scored raccoon down on impossibility, because raccoons already grasp things — but that reasoning optimized for conceptual absurdity while ignoring what actually determines whether a photoreal image succeeds. Render fidelity dominates. A goose cannot grasp, so every prompt asking one to hold an object forces the model to invent anatomy it has no reference for — and the gap gets filled with human-hand artifacts, the single most recognizable AI tell there is. Raccoon paws are genuinely hand-like and abundantly photographed doing exactly that. You get impossibility of behaviour without ever asking for impossibility of anatomy.

What is lost, and the replacement

The badge is gone — a costume prop cannot survive a photoreal brief. It was solving three real problems, so each needs a native replacement: continuity now comes from a permanent physical marking, the wordmark moves out of the artwork into the handle, caption and merch, and the per-episode premise engine becomes the act he is caught performing. See § 02 and § 03.

02

The canon — identity without costume

Everything below is fixed forever. The markers are the only way a viewer knows one photoreal raccoon from another, so they matter more here than any design decision in the previous version.

03

Two identities, one raccoon

Rufus runs in two modes. Same animal, same markers, same deadpan — what changes is whether he knows. That single variable is the character's whole arc, and it turns a gag account into a serial.

Why the two marks are the system

They are a matched pair, and each only works in its own mode. Ask for Rufus is what you say at a service desk — sincere on its face, a mild threat once you know him. It wasn't Rufus is a flat denial published directly beneath photographic evidence to the contrary, which only lands once he is actually committing things. Both are distinctive phrases rather than bare first names, which is what makes them protectable, and running both doubles your merch surface off one character.

Name under review — knockout search found a live conflict

A preliminary search of public records turned up BIG JOOS BRANDS, US serial 97867753: an active application in International Class 032 for tropical fruit juice, first use in commerce February 2023 — and a real operating brand, selling on its own site and on Amazon.

That is the textbook likelihood-of-confusion fact pattern: phonetically identical ("big juice" either way), same class, same goods. The wider field is crowded too — JOOSE is a flavoured malt beverage with an active brand behind it. BIG JOOCE should be treated as unavailable until a trademark attorney says otherwise.

Recommended replacement: BIG GLUG. It keeps every property that made the original work — "Big" still supplies potency without vice, it stays generic-feeling and simple, and it is highly sayable ("he's had the Big Glug"). Onomatopoeia reads instantly as drinking without being a homophone of "juice", which is what lifts it clear of the crowded field. A knockout search on it returned no beverage conflicts. That is not clearance; it is only the absence of an obvious blocker.

Everything below is unaffected by the name: the pouch design, the mechanic, the mode signalling and the caption formulas all hold whichever word ends up on the band.

The trigger: BIG JOOCE

What catnip is to a cat, a foil pouch of cheap orange drink is to Rufus. He finds them in bins. One pouch and the community man is gone.

Why this name, of everything considered

"Big" does the work alcohol would have done. The trigger had to read as potent without implying a vice — that was the needle. "Big" threads it: the drink is dangerous because it is large, not because it is intoxicating. Comic escalation at zero brand-safety cost, and it gives you a size lever to escalate with later.

"Jooce" is generic to read and coined to own. It parses as "juice" instantly, so the audience learns nothing — but the misspelling makes it a coined term rather than a descriptive one, which is the difference between a mark you can protect and a word anyone can use. As a composite, adjective plus coined noun is more distinctive than either half alone.

It passes the only normalisation test that matters: sayability in a comment. "He's had the Big Jooce." "Big Jooce hours." People type that unprompted, and that is the mechanic doing the marketing.

Known caution: "juice" carries drug slang, and "big" nudges toward it. The misspelling reads childlike rather than gym-slang and the visual is a children's pouch, which collapses the swagger — the gap between the bravado and the juice box is the joke. Be aware of it anyway. Separately, "Big ___" is crowded in beverage (Big Gulp, Big Red are strong registered marks); clear a search before printing anything.

The pouch — design carries the identity, not the type

Image models garble text, so the pouch must be identifiable with every letter unreadable. Specify it by shape and colour and let the lettering be incidental:

ElementLocked
FormatFoil drink pouch, child-size, slightly crumpled
BodyLight blue
MarkOne bold red band running horizontally across the middle, edge to edge, level — never diagonal
StrawYellow plastic, always inserted
LetteringBold white, set inside the red band — a bonus when it renders, never depended on

Light blue foil, horizontal red band, yellow straw is reproducible in every generation. The name lives where you control the typography: the handle, the captions, the merch. The audience learns the pouch by silhouette and colour, and learns what it is called from you.

Why light blue and why horizontal

Contrast. Red on light blue is a far stronger pairing than red on orange, which sits too close on the wheel to read cleanly at thumbnail size.

It survives Rogue mode. Night frames are lit by sodium streetlight — a warm orange wash. An orange pouch disappears into that palette; a light blue one is complementary and pops out of it. The trigger object has to be findable in exactly the frames where it matters most.

A horizontal band is a typographic home. A diagonal stripe cannot hold level type. A level band across the middle gives the wordmark a place to sit whenever a generation happens to render it, and gives your merch and packaging artwork a real lockup rather than an improvised one.

And it makes the fix trivial. Generations will render the lettering as gibberish — every test so far has. It does not matter, because a flat, level, solid-colour rectangle is the easiest possible compositing target: drop clean vector type onto the band in any editor and it reads as native packaging. A diagonal stripe would have needed perspective warping every time. Treat the garbled letters as a placeholder the band is holding open for you.

Why a prop, and why an invented one

It solves the identity problem the ear could not. Objects render reliably where anatomy does not, so the pouch carries continuity that the notch failed to. It is also the mode indicator, the transformation mechanic, and a third protectable mark all at once.

Invent the brand; never use a real one. Capri-Sun, Sunny D and Fanta are live trademarks. Building your character's core mechanic on someone else's mark — on a commercial account, with merchandise — is not nominative fair use, it invites a cease-and-desist from a company obliged to police its mark, and you can never sell the merch. A fictional off-brand gives you a mark you own, a visual you control across generations, and a joke that is funnier than the real thing.

Foil pouch, not a can. More distinctive in silhouette, readable at thumbnail size, absurd in raccoon paws — and a crushed pouch is instantly distinguishable from a full one, which is what makes it work as a mode signal.

Not alcohol, and not a drug analogue

Three reasons, in ascending order of importance. It is an advertiser exclusion and an age-gating risk, which permanently forfeits the brand-safety position. You cannot merchandise a character whose defining trait is drinking to cope. And most of all — it is less funny. Alcohol is a plausible cause of a personality change, and plausibility is fatal here. Catnip works because it is absurd and benign: a joke about cats being strange, not about cats having a problem. "He had one juice pouch and now he owns the cash machine" is comedy. "He drinks because he is sad" is a different show, and a sadder one.

The pivot is your tentpole

Somewhere around post thirty, Rufus realizes. That single image — the moment the confidence drops out of the face — is the most important post the account will ever make, and everything before it becomes setup in retrospect. Plan it, do not stumble into it. Afterwards the two modes alternate rather than replacing each other: the community man still turns up, and the question of which Rufus you are getting becomes the thing people follow for.

The mechanic worth exploiting

Most images work in either voice, and the caption alone decides which. The ATM frame captioned "assisting a customer with a routine transaction" is the community man; the same frame captioned "I'm inside" is Rogue. Once the audience understands both modes, posting an ambiguous image and letting the comments argue over which Rufus this is costs you nothing and generates exactly the discussion this format otherwise cannot buy.

Where the marks live

With the badge gone the wordmark cannot sit inside the artwork, and it should not — a watermark on a photoreal image reads as stock. Both marks live in the handle, the caption sign-off, and merchandise, which is how essentially all character IP works. Trademark rights build from use in commerce, not from the mark appearing in the picture. Over time the notched ear becomes the visual identifier, and a consistently used distinctive marking is the kind of thing that can mature into protectable trade dress.

Findings across six generations

Paws passed every single time. Real raccoon paws with claws, no hands, no fingers — six for six. That is the whole reason this character beat the goose, and it is now settled.

The camera block works once it is aggressive. Generation 001 ignored a politely-worded night brief and returned a bright, clean daylight interior. Rewritten to lead with darkness, name the light sources, and explicitly ask for a mediocre photo, it then delivered on every subsequent attempt — night sodium, dim strip-lit corridor, and flat municipal daylight all landed on request. Mode differentiation through lighting is reliable.

The notch will not come from a text prompt. Stated mid-paragraph it failed; stated first, in capitals, as the subject's one unmistakable feature, it still failed in two of three attempts and came back as a vaguely tattered ear in the third. Only a dedicated single-purpose edit pass produced a clean, thumbnail-legible wedge.

And edit passes are not free. Three stacked edits turned a convincing photograph into something visibly painterly — smeared surfaces, fur stripped of micro-detail. Generation loss compounds fast.

The ear notch is abandoned — four attempts, four failures

Stated mid-prompt: invisible. Stated first and in capitals: ignored. Requested subtly in an edit pass: invisible. Requested boldly in an edit pass: rendered as flat torn paper on one frame and a hallucinated wire loop floating beside the ear on another. There is no setting between "not there" and "obviously broken."

The generalisable finding: props render reliably, anatomy does not. Across every generation the models rendered cash machines, leaflets, noticeboards, keypads and a pair of spectacles flawlessly — and refused, every time, to modify the animal's body. They hold a strong prior for what a raccoon ear looks like and snap back to it. Identity must therefore be carried by something Rufus has, not something he is. See § 03.

The working pipeline

1. Generate the scene clean — text-to-image, mode-appropriate camera block, no anatomical marker instruction at all. This works reliably.
2. One edit pass only, for props and defects — adding or crushing the pouch, repairing an artifact. Object-level edits succeed where body-level edits fail; the flank repair in frame 1 worked first time.
3. Never a third pass. Three stacked edits turn a photograph into an oil painting.

The three failure modes to watch for

Human hands — the instant tell; reject the generation, never retouch it. Scale drift — models inflate him toward human size; he is a normal raccoon, roughly knee height. Over-polish — if it looks like a good photograph, it has failed. The reference posts in this genre succeed partly because they look slightly bad.

The one rule with legal weight

Block C asks for a generated scene, not an edit of a real photograph. Never feed a real street photo in as a base — that makes your output a derivative work of someone else's picture and forfeits every advantage this format has. If you want genuine locations, shoot the plates yourself and composite; that also creates real human authorship in the finished image, which is the only thing you can claim, since AI output alone carries no copyright.

Two structural notes

Episode 07 establishes a second visual register — grainy CCTV — which gives you variety without breaking realism, and doubles as proof for the running "it wasn't him" bit. Episode 13 gives the capybara its correct job: the foil that never reacts. Its meme equity is enormous and its personality is passive, which makes it a poor lead and a perfect witness.

06

Captions and the comment engine

Captions are written as denial and eyewitness testimony — never as narration or jokes. The image is the evidence; the caption is the alibi. This is also your original written contribution, which is the human authorship that matters for monetization eligibility.

Formula A — Rufus the Raccoon
[A civic bulletin. Present tense. He is reporting on his own good work.]
[One procedural detail he is proud of that the photograph flatly contradicts.]
[Optional: unsolicited advice for the public.]

Ask for Rufus.
Formula B — Rufus Gone Rogue
[One short line. Operational. No context, no explanation, no adjectives.]
[Optional second line — shorter than the first.]

It wasn't Rufus.

The same frame, both voices

Worked from the ATM image, to show how far the caption moves the meaning without touching the picture:

Rules that hold across both

  • Never explain the joke. The photo is the joke. The caption is either the alibi or the report.
  • Never break character. No emoji, no "lol", no meta-commentary about AI inside the caption.
  • Length is the tell. The community man over-explains because he thinks he did well. Rogue says as little as possible. If a Rogue caption runs past two lines, it is a community-man caption wearing the wrong sign-off.
  • He is never cruel and never armed. Rogue's revenge is procedural and property-scale — systems, machines, paperwork. Never a person, never a weapon; that is the brand-safety line and it does not move.
  • Sign-offs are load-bearing. Every caption ends with its mode's mark, verbatim, every time. Repetition is what builds a mark.
  • The vote line is the comment engine. Reference accounts in this genre run comment-to-like ratios around 0.002 — enormous reach, no community. Ask which mode people want next, or where he turns up. It needs no face and no voice.
  • Label the post as AI at upload. The label carries no ranking penalty; concealment is what costs you.
  • Alternate deliberately. Running the two modes in rotation is not only a story decision — it is the compositional variance that keeps you out of the mass-produced-formula bucket.
