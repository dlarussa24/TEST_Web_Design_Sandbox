Build Specification Companion to the Risk Playbook Rev. August 2026

The Compliant Build

A pop culture account that delivers the same product as an aggregator — the voice, the curation, the speed — while owning or licensing every frame it publishes.

In the aggregator model the borrowed media is load-bearing: remove it and there is no post. In this model the borrowed media is evidentiary — it illustrates a take that already stands on its own.

That single structural change is what satisfies the fair-use test, Instagram's April 2026 originality rule, and § 1202 simultaneously. Everything below is downstream of it.

The substitution test

Before anything ships, ask one question: could someone get the same value from the original source that they get from my post? If yes, you are substituting for the source and no amount of caption fixes it. If the post is only valuable because of your framing, you are commenting — and that is the whole ballgame, legally and algorithmically.

§ 00

Where to start — enter at rung 08, not rung 03

"Getting into clipping" describes three different businesses. Most people accidentally pick the one with the legal problem, because it is the visible one.

Business What you do Rights risk First $ You end up owning
Aggregator account Build an audience on borrowed media, monetize via brand deals High 6–12 months An audience
Paid clipper Cut a creator's own footage under their licence, paid per view Near zero Days A skill
Campaign operator Run clipping campaigns for creators, take a cut Near zero 2–4 months A business

Enter as a paid clipper, and treat it as a paid apprenticeship rather than as the business. Rung 08 of the ladder in § 02 is structurally different from every rung above it: the rights problem is solved by the business model instead of by your discipline. The creator funds a pool, publishes a brief, and hands you the footage with permission, because distribution is the entire point. No chain of title to trace, no registration gate, no fair-use argument to make. It also requires no audience, and it pays you to learn hook and retention mechanics — the one skill that makes formats A and B in § 01 work later.

What compliance actually remains — and it is not copyright

  • FTC disclosure is the real exposure. Being paid to distribute a brand's message is a material connection under 16 CFR § 255.5, and routing payment through a network operator rather than the brand changes nothing. Brand and clipper can both be liable, at roughly $50,120 per violation. Disclose in the clip, visibly, naming the sponsor — "#ad" alone can be insufficient.
  • The licence covers the footage, not the audio. Briefs almost never license music. Trending audio on a licensed clip is your gap, not the brand's.
  • Sub-clearance gaps inside the footage. A podcast clip where the host plays a trailer or a music video: they licensed you their podcast, not the third-party material inside it. Cut around it.
  • Read the brief as a licence, and keep it. Which platforms, personal accounts allowed, and does your right to keep the clip up survive the campaign ending? Screenshot it the day you accept — campaign pages get edited and deleted.
  • No account farms. Not a legal problem, an existential platform one — and precisely what unoriginal-content enforcement is tuned to catch.
The economics, accurately

Campaigns advertise $1–$5 per 1,000 views, with listings from $0.20 to $6. The blended actual, across roughly $2.58M in payouts against 6.6B views, is about $0.39 per 1,000. A realistic first month is $0–$80; a serious operator posting 10–20 clips a day lands $400–$1,500/month. Campaign owners can reject clips after the views land, and disputes can hold payouts up to 90 days. Read it as tuition, not income — that gap is exactly why the goal is to graduate off it.

The 90-day path

Week 1–2
Pick narrow
Two or three campaigns. Choose creators whose long-form you would actually watch — your judgment about what lands is the asset, and it does not function on material that bores you. Screenshot every brief.
Week 3–6
Volume, instrumented
10+ clips a day, with a sheet: source timestamp, hook used, first-three-seconds framing, retention, payout. You are not farming views, you are building a private dataset on what makes a cold viewer stay. Disclose on every paid clip from day one so it becomes reflex.
Week 7–12
Flip to the other side of rung 08
Approach creators with large long-form libraries and no clipping presence — mid-size podcasters, streamers, coaches — and offer to run their campaign. You set the brief and own the licensing terms. Now you are the licensor rather than the licensee, paid on the spread rather than per view, and the disclosure discipline becomes a selling point to any brand with a legal department.

Then sequence the account rather than abandoning it. Clipping income funds the things that make the model in § 01 lawful — an editorial stills subscription, a viral-clip licence, a camera for Format E — and the retention skill is exactly what formats A and B run on. Enter through the door where the licence arrives with the footage, treat the money as tuition, and use it to buy your way into owning something.

§ 01

Five post formats that carry the product legally

These are the only shapes you need. A healthy feed runs roughly 40% A, 25% C, 20% D, 10% B, 5% E — heavy on the two formats that cost nothing and carry no rights exposure, with the expensive formats reserved for the posts that deserve them.

A
The Take Card

You design the graphic. Typographic news card, list, ranking, poll, or bracket in your own brand system. No third-party image at all, or a link out to the source instead of an embed. This is already how the largest pop-culture accounts carry their news posts.

Rights none needed Cost minutes Originality 100%
Rebuilt from the audit "Jerry Springer was the comment section before social media existed" was a broadcast still plus a one-liner. The still is NBCU's; the joke is yours. Set the line as a card in your type system and you keep everything that made the post work, at zero exposure.
B
Voiceover Commentary

Your voice runs through the clip, stopping it to react, contextualize, and argue. The Hosseinzadeh posture. Note carefully: voiceover strengthens a fair-use argument, it does not create a license — so pair it with a base clip sourced from § 02.

Rights license the base Cost 15–30 min Originality high
Rebuilt from the audit The Jim Lee drawing demo ran as a silent clip with "Stan Lee's voice is genuinely healing me" underneath. Same clip, licensed, with you talking over it about what he's actually doing with the pen — now it's a different work with a different purpose.
C
Native Collab & Repost

Instagram's own Collab post and repost button. Authorship stays with the creator, the post appears on both grids, and you add your caption. No download, no re-upload, no copy made, and no chance of substituting your mark for theirs.

Rights platform-licensed Cost free § 1202 risk zero
Rebuilt from the audit The capybara clip and the NYC ladybug video are both third-party UGC re-uploaded into a house-branded frame. Collab with the original poster instead: they get reach, you get the post, and the clip never leaves their account.
D
The Permissioned Clip

Ask the owner, get a written yes, post it with the credit they asked for and your take on top. Free, per-item, and the single highest-leverage habit in the whole model — it converts an infringement into a license with one message.

Rights express grant Cost one DM Scales to 3–5 posts/day
Rebuilt from the audit Tweet screenshots are the easiest possible ask — creators almost always say yes to a pop-culture page with reach. Template in § 03.
E
Owned Footage

Your own camera. Premieres and conventions issue press credentials to accounts your size; screenings, launches, pop-ups, and street interviews need nothing but showing up. Slowest to produce and the only content a competitor structurally cannot take from you.

Rights you own it Cost time Also a licensable asset
Why it matters disproportionately Owned footage is the only format that makes you a rights holder. It converts the account from a cost center that borrows into a library that licenses out.
§ 02

The sourcing ladder

Ranked by preference, not convenience. Work down it and stop at the first rung that yields the asset — the order genuinely matters, because each step down adds cost, latency, or residual risk.

01
You shot it or made it
Free, perpetual, exclusive, and licensable to others. no clearance
02
Native Collab or repost
The platform's own sharing mechanics. No copy is made, so no reproduction right is touched. platform-licensed
03
Written permission from the actual owner
One message, kept forever. Trace to the real owner — a grant from someone who themselves reposted it is worth nothing. express grant
04
Official press and EPK channels
Studio media sites, label and brand newsrooms, artist press kits, official trailers. Distributed expressly for editorial use — read the terms, they usually forbid alteration. editorial licence
05
Public domain and US federal works
Federal government works are uncopyrightable under 17 U.S.C. § 105 — NASA, the Library of Congress, the National Archives. Deep archive value for anything retrospective. free to use
06
Creative Commons
Wikimedia Commons, Flickr CC. Honour the licence exactly — attribution string, and check whether the variant permits commercial use, because your account is commercial. conditional
07
Paid editorial libraries
Getty and AP editorial subscriptions for celebrity stills; Jukin, ViralHog and Storyful for viral clips. Real money, and the only way to run agency photography safely. paid licence
08
Brand-authorized clipping programs
The source material arrives with permission because the rights holder wants the distribution — and often pays for it. Requires FTC disclosure. licensed + paid
Chain of title

The most common way a careful account still gets caught: getting permission from the wrong person. The account that posted it is usually not the photographer, the production company, or the agency. Ask "did you shoot this?" before you ask "can I use this?" — and if the answer is vague, treat it as a no.

§ 03

The permission workflow

A grant is only useful if it covers what you actually do. Six elements make one durable — anything missing is a gap you will not notice until it matters.

Element Why it has to be explicit
Who is grantingThey must be the owner, not a reposter. This is the element people skip.
Exactly whatIdentify the specific asset by link. "Your stuff" is not a licence.
Which platformsInstagram and TikTok and anywhere else you cross-post.
For how longPerpetual, or you inherit a deletion obligation you will forget.
Commercial useYour account is commercial. A grant for "personal use" does not cover you.
Credit formatTheir preferred handle, spelled their way, in the place they want it.

Storing it

Screenshot every yes, including the handle and the date, and file it under the asset. A grant you cannot produce two years later is, evidentially, a grant you never had.

§ 04

Pre-flight gate

Run every post through this before it ships. Two answers are hard stops; the rest are correctable. Nothing here is stored or transmitted.

Do I own, or hold written permission for, every visual asset in this post — or is it a native Collab, repost, public-domain, CC, or EPK asset?
Have I preserved the original creator's mark or handle, rather than replacing it with my own branding?
Would this post still be worth posting if the third-party asset were removed?
Does my commentary discuss the source itself, rather than sitting beside it?
Is an identifiable private individual named, shown in distress, or the subject of a factual claim?
If anything was paid, gifted, affiliate-linked, or campaign-submitted — is the disclosure clear and conspicuous in the post?
Is the audio from the Meta Sound Collection, separately licensed, or original?
Is every asset in this post recorded in the source log?
Awaiting Answer all eight to get a verdict.

    § 06

    Monetizing without unwinding it

    Right of publicity is where compliant accounts still get caught, because the exposure appears at the moment you start earning. The line is not about the celebrity — it is about whether their identity is selling something.

    Revenue Safe Condition
    Brand partnership, celebrity not featuredYesClear and conspicuous disclosure in the post
    Affiliate linksYesDisclose the material connection, every time
    Newsletter and paid subscriptionYesSame rights rules apply to the content inside it
    Merch using your brand or your own catchphrasesYesMust be your original IP, not a lifted line
    Licensing out your own footageYesRegister it — that is what makes it enforceable
    Merch with a celebrity's face, name, or catchphraseNoRight of publicity; no newsworthiness defence for goods
    Sponsored post where a celebrity appears to endorseNoAdvertising gets the least First Amendment protection
    Selling prints or edits of third-party photosNoStraight infringement, plus the publicity claim
    § 07

    The bio

    The audited account's bio — "DM for biz/credit/removal" — is a written confession that rights are resolved after publication. Do not carry that formula over. What you want instead says the same operational thing without conceding anything:

    Replacement bio 
    [Name] — pop culture, with a take
Original commentary · licensed & permissioned media
Partnerships and rights: [email]

    Three deliberate changes. "Original commentary" states the thing Instagram's April 2026 rule is scanning for. "Licensed & permissioned" asserts a rights posture instead of apologising for the absence of one. And routing rights contact to email rather than DMs gives you a record, a place to attach the licence file, and — critically — stops you advertising removal-on-demand as a standing offer.

    § 08

    Appendix — migrating an account that already exists

    If you are fixing a live account rather than starting clean, order matters. The cheapest changes stop new exposure immediately; the back catalogue can be worked in parallel.

    Day 1
    Bio and template
    Rewrite the bio per § 07. Strip your own handle out of the post template wherever the underlying asset is someone else's — that one change stops new § 1202 exposure accruing, at zero cost.
    Week 1–4
    Shift the mix
    Move new posts onto formats A and C. Target 60% original inside a month — that is also the window Instagram's rolling 30-day aggregator assessment runs on, so reach should respond.
    Week 2–8
    Triage the back catalogue
    Sort by risk class and work top-down: agency and paparazzi stills first, then studio poster art, then broadcast stills. Delete or replace. Leave the tweet screenshots that preserved original handles for last — they are the least exposed.
    Week 4–12
    Build the pipeline
    Permission templates into a saved-replies list, source log running from day one, and a standing habit of shooting your own footage at anything you attend.
    What deletion does and doesn't do

    Removing an infringing post stops ongoing distribution and helps on the equities. It does not erase liability that has already accrued — the claim exists from the moment of publication, and the limitations period runs three years from discovery, not from posting. Clean up because it reduces future exposure and improves your posture, not because it makes the past disappear.

Right-of-publicity, defamation, and privacy rules vary substantially between states, and fair use is decided case by case on facts this document cannot know.
